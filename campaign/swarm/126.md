# Idea 126: a message hidden in the solved K1-K3 text (acrostics and skip readings)

**Verdict: dead in scope.** No reading of the solved text looks more like English than the same
reading taken from the text with its word order shuffled.

## The idea

In 2005 Sanborn said he left instructions in the earlier text that refer to later text. In 2006 he
said the answers to the first three passages hold clues to the fourth. Letter codes of the war years
hid messages in the first or n-th letters of words. Earlier rounds used such letters only as K4 keys
(attack 85, idea 106, round 9), and all of those are dead. Nobody here had asked whether the letters
read as a message. That question needs no K4 and no cipher model.

## What was run

`attacks/126_hidden_readings_k1k3.py` with helpers in `kryptos/idea126_hidden.py`.

* **Texts.** K1, K2, K3, the Morse text, and K1-K3 joined. There are 24 styles: carved or corrected
  spelling, X (and K3's Q) kept or dropped, and K2 ending ID BY ROWS or X LAYER TWO.
* **Readings.** 44,876 readings of 12+ letters, each forwards and backwards:
  * the first, second, third and last letters of words, and the running letters;
  * every k-th item for k up to 40, from every start;
  * the K2 coordinates used as repeating step counts (as digits and as numbers);
  * the first letter, last letter and each column of the plaintext laid under the 25 carved cipher rows;
  * sentence initials and finals;
  * doubled letters.
* **Scores.** Seven of them: big-model quadgram mean, best 20-letter quadgram window (raw, and as a
  z-score against reference controls), word coverage, longest dictionary word, method-word count, and
  the longest stretch that splits wholly into common English words.
* **Families.** The readings fall into three families: letters taken from words, letters of the running
  text, and the carved rows. The statistic is the largest value per family and score, which gives
  21 maxima. These are combined through a Gumbel tail per maximum.
* **Controls.** 500 reference and 1,500 test texts with word order shuffled inside each section. The
  p-value pays for every reading.

## Numbers

* **K1-K3:** combined 1.45, against a control median of 1.44 and a control maximum of 3.63. 732 of 1,500
  controls score at least as high, so **p = 0.49**.
* **Nearest channels.** Two channels come closest, but neither survives paying for 21 channels:
  * carved-row column 1 read backwards (HLHIETRDEFLETEACHOKDCSTOE) has a 10-letter common-word run
    (controls: median 7, max 13; p = 0.053);
  * every 20th letter has a quadgram window of -4.07 (...NOTAREDTEMONTHANTAIEWA...; p = 0.055).
* **Acrostic family:** the longest common-word run is 9 letters (controls: median 10, max 18).
* **Read by eye:** the misspelt letters QUAEU, the raised YAR, and the letters next to each X. Nothing
  there.
* **Planted.** Each plant is a 20-letter instruction in a K1-K3-length text:
  * word initials, every word: SHIFTEACHROWBYTHEKEY;
  * word initials, every third word: ADDTHECLOCKTIMETOROW;
  * second letters, every word: READLAYERTWOEASTWARD.

  All 3 put a reading holding the full message first. Two beat all 1,000 test controls
  (p = 1/1001). The third has p = 2/1001: one control beat it through the method-word count.

## Scope limits

The test finds a hidden message of about 20+ letters made of common words. It would miss:

* a shorter message, or one made of rare words;
* a key-like letter string;
* meaning carried by whole words.

The ciphertexts, the tableau panel and other layouts were not read. In letter-skip readings, chance
alone makes common-word runs of 13-21 letters, which sets this limit.

## What was surprising

The plain quadgram scores could not find a 20-letter plant among 44,876 readings at all (0/3 in
tuning). Chance letter salad such as AREDTEMONTHANTAIEWA scores like English on quadgrams. Only the
common-word run separates real instructions from noise.

The first way of combining the 21 maxima (mean and sd) let heavy-tailed channels beat a plant. It was
replaced by a Gumbel fit; the plants were never changed.
