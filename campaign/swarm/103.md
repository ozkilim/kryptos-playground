# Idea 103: matrix codes on rectangles that hold 26 or 27 symbols

**Verdict: dead in scope.** No decryption came out for K4 at all, and none for the shuffled K4s.

## The idea

Sanborn (2005) said he used "matrix codes" from Scheidt. Playfair, two-square and four-square were logged dead
only because a 5x5 square gives 25 letters and K4 has 26. A square drawn as a 2x13 or 13x2 rectangle keeps all
26; with the carved ? there are 98 symbols = 49 pairs and 27 = 3x9 cells. These ciphers work on fixed pairs,
so the clue letters stay in place, and two-square can leave a letter as it is (K to K).

## What was run (`attacks/103_matrix_codes_on_rectangles.py`, 1.3 minutes on 8 cores)

Pairings: letter i with letter i + w in blocks of 2w, w = 1..48, every block start (2,352 pairings; counting
from the ? only moves the start by one, so both are in the list).

1. **Part 1, no key.** Any one-to-one table of letter pairs. Equal cipher pairs must hide equal plain pairs,
   and one plain pair cannot give two cipher pairs.
2. **Two-square, no key.** Every clue letter gives a "same column" condition inside one square and a "same
   row" link between the squares. A column holds R letters, a row holds C, two letters cannot share a cell.
   If the clue letters break that, no two squares of that shape exist. Both output orders. One square above
   the other is the same cipher turned on its side, and the grid list holds both shapes.
3. **Two-square, listed squares.** 666,993 alphabets (A-Z, KRYPTOS, dictionary keyword alphabets and their two
   transposition-mixed forms), by rows or by columns, ? first or last on 27 cells. Each square is filtered on
   its own column conditions; the two survivor lists are hash-joined on the row links.
4. **Four-square**, plain squares A-Z or KRYPTOS by rows: each full clue pair fixes one cell in each cipher
   square. First check that no two letters land on one cell, then test every listed square.
5. Playfair on a rectangle moves every letter to another cell. K4 has plain K = cipher K at letter 74. Not run.

Steps 3 and 4 ran on the Part 1 survivors with 6 or more full clue pairs (465 pairings for K4).

* Ordinary pairs (w = 1) pass Part 1 at both starts, but **no two squares of any kind** fit them on 2x13, 13x2,
  3x9 or 9x3, in either output order. Example on 2x13 (start 1, textbook order): the clue pairs ST and AS force V, S and A into one
  column of two cells.
* Run on all 2,352 pairings, the no-key two-square test leaves nothing below w = 29 on 2x13, 13x2 and 3x9, and
  only w = 16 and w = 27 on 9x3. From w = 29 up some clue letters have no partner, so the test weakens.
* The 18 settings that some squares could fit are all at w = 41-45 (clue block one paired with clue block two).
  No listed square pair fits them.
* Four-square with A-Z / KRYPTOS plain squares is impossible at w = 1 for any cipher squares.
* Bean's seriated result is reproduced: w = 7 fails at 13 of 14 starts (cipher pair BQ would stand for two
  plain pairs), w = 21 at 40 of 42.

**Planted: 4/4**, each found among all 666,993 alphabets, read back letter for letter and ranked first:
two-square side by side on 2x13 (PALIMPSEST, SHADOW); one above the other on 2x13 (LODESTONE, COMPASS), found
as the turned 13x2 form; two-square on 3x9 with the ?, w = 5 (ABSCISSA, MEDUSA); four-square on 3x9 with the ?
enciphered as symbol 0 (BERLIN, ILLUSION; plain squares KRYPTOS and A-Z). Look-alike words (SHODDY for SHADOW)
gave near-copies of the same message (80+ of 97 letters); no other joined setting read as English.
The no-key tests never rejected a true message in 1,800 random-square trials (150 of them in the unit test).

## Scope limits

* Two-square here means: one plain letter looked up in each square, cipher = the other two corners, either
  order, both squares the same shape. The no-key result is exact where it kills. Where it does not (18
  settings at w = 41-45, and pairings with fewer than 6 full clue pairs, mostly w >= 29) only the listed
  squares were tried.
* Four-square needs plain squares A-Z or KRYPTOS by rows. Other plain squares are covered by Part 1 only.
* Squares that change during the message, nulls, or a transposition before or after are not covered.
* Clue positions are taken as exact.
* "Tested" (193.5 billion) counts single-square checks; the joins cover every pair of listed squares.

## Surprising

The key list hardly mattered. The clue words repeat letters (E four times, T three times), and a 2-row or 3-row
square has too few cells per column to hold what those repeats demand. That alone closes two-square on ordinary
pairs for every possible square, which is stronger than the dictionary run the idea asked for. The shuffled
texts die the same way, so this is a fact about the clue words, not a statistical signal.
