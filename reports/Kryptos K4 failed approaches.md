# Mapping thirty-five years of K4 dead ends

Every single textbook cipher applied the ordinary way to K4 is dead. That covers periodic Vigenère, Beaufort and Quagmire at short periods, autokey, Hill up to 5×5, Playfair and the 5×5 fractionation family, monoalphabetic substitution, pure transposition, and columnar transposition of width 11 or less combined with any periodic key of length 26 or less. These eliminations rest on the 24 released crib letters (EASTNORTHEAST at positions 22–34, BERLINCLOCK at 64–74) and on K4's flat index of coincidence (about 0.036). They have been reproduced by at least four independent 2021–2026 searches with planted-cipher validation, and I re-derived the core period result locally. Running keys from the obvious texts are equally dead: K1–K3, Carter's *Tomb of Tut-ankh-Amen*, the King James Bible, the founding documents, and Gutenberg-scale English corpora all score at chance. So do Berlin-clock lamp keys, the M-94, Chaocipher at the tested scale, rotor machines, and LFSRs. What remains open has one of three shapes. Some families fall in crib-undecidable parameter zones, where 24 letters give at most one constraint. Some lie just past the searched bounds: double columnar, wide or irregular routes, Hill ≥6×6, turning grilles, and arbitrary-alphabet Gromark. The rest break the shared assumption behind nearly every ledger, that crib positions map cleanly onto the 97 carved letters: nulls, "masking", visual or physical steps. Sanborn and Scheidt have never published a list of excluded ciphers. They have said K4 uses a different, masked process from K1–K3, has more than one stage, is in English, may not be "a math solution", and contains deliberate errors. They have also said that BERLINCLOCK matches NYPVTTMZFPK "one-to-one". Every claimed solution so far has failed in one of three ways: the cribs are missing or misplaced, the key was back-solved from a chosen plaintext, or the method cannot be reproduced. The 2025 Smithsonian discovery of the plaintext did not include the method. Since June 2026, Paradigm's $1 exact-match hash checker is the only ground truth, and no claim has publicly passed it.

## Twenty-four crib letters already kill every short-period polyalphabetic

The most reliable negatives in the record are not opinions. They are consistency proofs. Each crib position fixes one keystream value under a given cipher family. Any family whose key repeats, or is generated from a few parameters, must reproduce all 24 values at once. On the standard alphabet the implied Vigenère keystream is `BLZCDCYYGCKAZ` (22–34) and `MUYKLGKORNA` (64–74). The implied Beaufort keystream is `JLJODEGKUKKKLOCGGBGOKTRU`. I re-derived both and checked them against the published tables ([keithadler/kryptos SEARCH.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/keithadler/kryptos/main/SEARCH.md); [Schridde, NumberWorld](https://www.numberworld.blog/2017/03/kryptos-cipher-part-2.html)). A local check confirms the community claim: **no period from 1 to 48 is consistent for Vigenère or Beaufort on A–Z except 27, 28 and 29**, and those pass only because no two crib positions share a residue at those periods ([gereon/kryptos](https://github.com/gereon/kryptos)). The same check finds **two self-encipherments: S→S at position 33 and K→K at position 74**. Those alone rule out every cipher that cannot map a letter to itself: Enigma-style reflector machines, Porta, standard Playfair, and other reciprocal schemes ([keithadler](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/keithadler/kryptos/main/SEARCH.md); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)). The index of coincidence is near random, 0.0361 in Bean and keithadler and 0.0363 in my count of the same transcription (pin the canonical text). English runs about 0.066, so pure transposition, and monoalphabetic substitution followed by transposition, are both out ([gereon](https://github.com/gereon/kryptos); [Bean 2021](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)).

The strongest structural result is that **periodic substitution with fully arbitrary, independent alphabets per key class is impossible at periods 1–7, 9, 10, 14, 15, 17, 21 and 25**. That family contains every Quagmire and every keyword. Three ledgers reach this independently ([gereon](https://github.com/gereon/kryptos); [Barneyjm/kryptos-k4](https://github.com/Barneyjm/kryptos-k4); [zeyeteam ledger](https://github.com/zeyeteam-debug/kryptos-k4-eliminations)). Gereon extends the impossible list to 34, 42, 43 and 45. The other periods are not eliminated. They are *crib-undecidable*: the cribs place too few constraints on them to test anything, which is a different status from "tested and failed". A negatives database must record that distinction explicitly.

The table shows how lopsided the evidence is. Bean's statistical work points to letter-for-letter substitution with no transposition: 11 repeated vertical bigrams at width 21 (p ≈ 1/6,750 under shuffling, which I reproduced), "minor difference" clusters (p ≈ 1/5,520), and many plain-minus-cipher values that are multiples of 5 (p ≈ 1/1,470) ([Bean](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). Yet the substitution families that would naturally produce those signals are the most thoroughly killed. Bean himself warns that his tests were chosen after looking at the data, so they count as leads, not proof. Two of them have never been explained by any eliminated family: the width-21 structure and the multiples-of-5 pattern, which suggests a Polybius-style or conjugated-matrix bifid where "nothing was found".

## Transposition and running keys die only inside the searched box

Combined transposition-plus-substitution and running keys are the families where scope matters most. Here a negatives database is most likely to overstate what is closed. The largest single search is keithadler's. It covers **every column order at widths 2–11**, read down, up or alternating, forward and inverse, key before or after, key length 1–26: **44M column orders and 110 billion combinations**, plus pruned searches at widths 12–14. All were ruled out ([keithadler SEARCH.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/keithadler/kryptos/main/SEARCH.md)). The same search treats crib positions as pre-transposition plaintext positions. Every transposition elimination below inherits that assumption.

Sanborn's 2006 admission that he dropped an X from K2 "for aesthetic reasons" turned "ID BY ROWS" into "LAYER TWO". ## Claimed solutions fail one of three tests, now enforced by a $1 hash

Reliable outlets name almost no individual claimants. What is documented is a volume problem and a consistent failure pattern. By 2010, Sanborn's gatekeeper checked a single letter ("What do you have in letter-position 27?"), and "no one has had more than two letters right", which is chance level ([NYT 2010](http://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21code.html)). Position 27 falls inside NORTHEAST (released 2020), so under 1-based indexing the check letter was O, a letter no submitter had found in a decade. That reading is my inference. Gatekeeping then moved through a 10-character website (2010), a $50 Western Union fee (c.2012), and an AI auto-responder (2025) ([NYT 2014](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html); [SciAm](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)). Sanborn reports "tens of thousands" of submissions ([Open Letter 2025](https://elonka.com/kryptos/OpenLetterAug2025.html)).

The **AI wave** is the largest rejected class. From early 2025 Sanborn's inbox filled with Grok 3 and ChatGPT "solutions" ("Cracked it in days with Grok 3"; "no errors 100% cracked"). Sanborn's reply was "AI lies, and does not have enough info" ([Gizmodo 2025-03-07](https://gizmodo.com/chatbots-have-convinced-idiots-that-they-cracked-the-code-on-a-mysterious-sculpture-in-the-cias-backyard-2000573216); [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2025/03/08/cia-kryptos-sculptor-disgusted-by-flood-of-wrong-ai-solutions.html)). Paradigm said in June 2026 that AI-assisted attempts had "exploded" but "none have succeeded yet" ([Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)). Aaron Toponce's running gist, "No, ChatGPT didn't solve Kryptos 4" (2024–2026), is the best catalogue. Most LLM outputs place EAST, NORTHEAST, BERLIN and CLOCK at the wrong positions or produce the wrong length. Thiago Avelar's December 2025 Claude-derived GROMARK "solution" places the cribs correctly, but its key is labelled "obtained by reverse engineering", so the round-trip check is circular ([Toponce gist](https://gist.github.com/atoponce/1cd01f40786ab21af011f0d6257fba0c)).

The **back-solved key** is the subtler failure, and it also affects human claimants. SolveKryptos.com (Matt Lacy, June–July 2026) published a reconstructed plaintext and a Quagmire III variant with a helper keystream "read from the back of the sculpture" and a one-bit positional gate. The site itself concedes that only about 24 of 97 positions are confirmed and that most key values are "back-solved from the plaintext". It does not claim to have passed Paradigm's checker ([SolveKryptos](https://solvekryptos.com/solution)). The same objection disposes of the 2016 kryptosfan "full solution" claim. There a commenter demonstrated that an arbitrary passkey can generate any target text ([kryptosfan](https://kryptosfan.wordpress.com/2014/12/07/krypto-fans-where-are-we-at/)). It also disposes of the "Scott" near-miss, in which the first 11 letters of K1, used as a Quagmire III key, reportedly yielded BERLIN at 64–69. That fit did not extend, and keithadler's exact search now excludes the general case ([Schridde](https://www.numberworld.blog/2017/03/kryptos-cipher-part-2.html)). Self-published "definitive" papers fall in the same unverified bin: Glanz 2025, Salcedo Novoa 2026, and figshare and Medium hoax pieces ([asren aggregator](https://asren-aggregator.asren.net/Record/Manara_ce2fbb5d0997af3dcce98a9fbb71a72e); [Academia.edu](https://independent.academia.edu/MSalcedoNovoa)).

The **2025 Smithsonian find** is the most prominent "solution". It was a discovery, not a decryption. On 2 September 2025 Richard Byrne photographed Sanborn's papers at the Archives of American Art. Jarett Kobek spotted cut-and-taped plaintext strips that Sanborn had made in 1990 for CIA review, and the pair emailed Sanborn on 3 September ([SciAm 2025-10-22](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-the-cias-kryptos-sculpture-gave-up-its-final-secret/); [Zona Motel](https://zonamotel.substack.com/p/interview-kryptos-k4-uncovered-jarett)). Kobek: "There's no way on earth that this is a cryptographic solve." Elonka Dunin: "If they don't have the method, it's not solved" ([RR Auction](https://content.rrauction.com/kryptos-k4-discovered-not-solved-heres-what-actually-happened/)). The files are sealed until 2075. RR Auction lot #2001 sold for **$962,500**. It included "the original coding system for K4", handwritten plaintexts used for screen cutting, and an alternate 1988 K4 plaintext now called K5. No part of the method was photographed or described, and RR did not verify the materials ([RR lot 2001](https://www.rrauction.com/auctions/lot-detail/350761607302001-the-complete-secrets-of-kryptos-jim-sanborns-private-archive/)). Paradigm revealed itself as the buyer on 12 June 2026. Its verifier works as follows: Sanborn typed the plaintext on a dedicated machine; its SHA-256 went to Google Cloud KMS for an HMAC tag; the machine was wiped; and guesses cost $1 ([Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)). The verifier is an **exact-match oracle with no partial credit**, so it cannot guide hill-climbing. No submission counts have been published. Paradigm's ten-puzzle CTF (Quagmire III stacks, columnar, double columnar and spiral transpositions, a 3×3 Hill, running keys) reflects the designers' idea of "Kryptos-like" ciphers. It is not evidence about K4, because Paradigm does not know the method ([saul-g write-up](https://github.com/saul-g/kryptos-ctf-2026)). It is still useful as a validation suite: a K4 pipeline that cannot solve PK1–PK10 should not be trusted to produce negatives.

## Genuinely untested ground sits in three distinct zones

Once overlapping ledgers are merged, what remains open falls into three zones that call for different tools. The first zone is **crib-undecidable, not untested**. These are parameter regions where 24 letters supply at most one constraint, so a consistency proof is impossible and only language-model scoring with random-ciphertext calibration can decide. Examples: arbitrary-alphabet periodic substitution at periods 8, 11–13, 16, 18–20, 22–24, 26 and above; Quagmire I/II at 13, 16, 19, 20, 23, 24, 26; Quagmire IV at 8, 13, 16, 19, 20, 23, 24, 26; and repeating keys of length ≥27. keithadler's annealer recovered planted Quagmire I and II at several of these lengths and found K4 no better than random. That evidence is partial: it is uninformative for two free alphabets at L=8, for Quagmire II at L≥20, and for Quagmire I at L=26 ([keithadler](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/keithadler/kryptos/main/SEARCH.md)). The 472 two-layer key-length pairs with too many unknowns belong here too.

The second zone is **just past the searched box**, and the ledgers themselves flag these. Quagmire III in Beaufort form at L=13 and 18 timed out. Keyed double columnar beyond width pairs 2–6 or beyond clue-word keys is open. So are Myszkowski and disrupted columnar at width ≥10, route ciphers other than columns and rotations, turning grilles beyond 42% of one variant, Hill ≥6×6 and the two untestable 4×4 alignments, and 6×6 fractionation. Gromark and lagged-Fibonacci keys with arbitrary alphabets remain open: Bean's 39 base-10 primers and four base-8 primers were only annealed, and keithadler's digit-key result covers only A–Z, KRYPTOS and dictionary alphabets. A full dictionary-by-dictionary Chaocipher run is also untested ([zeyeteam](https://github.com/zeyeteam-debug/kryptos-k4-eliminations); [Bean](https://ecp.ep.liu.se/index.php/histocrypt/article/download/153/109)). So are the pre-1997 Weltzeituhr city-plate order and per-letter lookups. That is the single most theme-motivated gap now that Sanborn has named that clock. Typo-tolerant transposition and stacked layers with long keys are also open. One family exists that no source mentions testing at all: a 5×5 Polybius or conjugated-matrix scheme adapted to 26 letters. Bean's multiples-of-5 signal points straight at it, and he reports only that "nothing was found".

The third zone is **outside the shared model**. This is where the designers' words point and where no ledger has tooling. Nearly every elimination assumes the carved 97 letters with no nulls, crib positions aligned to ciphertext positions or to a pre-transposition plaintext, the "?" belonging to K3, and an English scorer. Scheidt's "masking technique may not be known", Sanborn's "visual systems", "who says it is even a math solution?", deliberate errors, and code words inside the plaintext all describe systems that could break those assumptions. Examples: irregular hand masks or null patterns, a step read off the sculpture's physical layout ("LAYER TWO", folding, the tableau's anomalies), or a plaintext that is itself a code. Sanborn's "one-to-one" BERLINCLOCK statement constrains this zone usefully. Whatever the mask is, it apparently preserves position over 64–74. The cleanest future test is a **K4/K5 depth attack** once Paradigm releases K5. If shared "coded words in the same positions" means identical ciphertext under a shared positional key, that release alone could expose the keystream structure.

For the database itself, each negative should record its family and exact parameter ranges, the alphabet set, the crib indexing and "?" convention, whether cribs were treated as pre- or post-transposition, its error tolerance, whether planted-cipher recovery was demonstrated, its shuffled or random-ciphertext baseline, and its count of independent replications. The research behind this report has gaps of its own. Reddit, groups.io (HTTP 402) and the old Yahoo archive could not be read. The NSA 2007 summary (HTTP 403), Bean's k4testing repository and the per-family Kryptosfan pages were not opened. Every 2026 ledger figure is the authors' own claim.

## Conclusion

The record overturns a common assumption: K4 is not "unsolved because nobody tried the obvious". The obvious has been closed with proofs, and the closure is unusually trustworthy, because independent ledgers agree on the same impossible-period list and the core keystream facts reproduce in a few lines of code. The real lesson is about where effort has *not* gone. Compute has been spent almost entirely inside one model: position-aligned cribs, no nulls, standard reading order, English scoring. Meanwhile every primary statement from the designers about masking, visual systems, non-mathematical steps and deliberate errors points at precisely the assumptions that model hard-codes. A new search that merely widens a column-width bound buys little. One that varies the alignment model buys more, and so does one that targets the unexplained width-21 and multiples-of-5 statistics.

The ground truth has also changed. Since 2026 the plaintext exists, sealed, behind an exact-match $1 oracle, and the method exists in Paradigm's envelopes. A K4 attack is now strictly a method-recovery problem with a binary verifier. A negatives database is therefore most valuable if it tracks exactly what each failed model *assumed*, not just what it tried. The K5 release is the one scheduled event that could turn several open zones into decidable ones overnight.
