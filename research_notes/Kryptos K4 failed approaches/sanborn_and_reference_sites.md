# Sanborn / Scheidt Statements Constraining K4, and Dead Ends on Curated Reference Sites

Scope: statements by Jim Sanborn and Ed Scheidt that rule out or constrain K4, plus dead ends listed on the main reference sites (Elonka Dunin's pages and FAQ, Wikipedia, CIA.gov, NSA's declassified K1-K3 solution). Research date: 2026-11-15.

Method note: wired.com and web.archive.org could not be fetched directly with WebFetch. I pulled the original 2005, 2006, 2010 and 2014 Wired articles, and the 2010, 2014 and 2020 NYT articles, as Wayback Machine snapshots using curl (URLs in the form `https://web.archive.org/web/<year>id_/<original URL>`). The quotes below come from those snapshots, so they are primary text. Citations give the original URL; the archived copy is at the web.archive.org prefix plus that URL. The NSA FOIA PDF (Black Vault mirror) was downloaded and converted to text with pdftotext. The OCR is noisy but readable.

## Q1. What has Sanborn (or Scheidt) said K4 is NOT, or which methods are not used?

### Takeaway
Sanborn has never published a list of excluded cipher types. The firm constraints are these:
- K4 uses a different process from K1-K3, and it hides English letter statistics (a "masking" layer).
- The scheme includes modified or invented systems of Sanborn's own, some of them "visual" rather than machine-decipherable.
- It may not be a purely mathematical solution.
- It contains deliberate errors.
- K4's plaintext is not the end, because a riddle and K5 follow.
- Finding the plaintext in the archive (2025) does not count as solving it: Sanborn says the "key" was not found.

### Cited Findings

**Different method from K1-K3; masking**
- Scheidt (Wired interview, published 2005-01-21) on how many processes Kryptos uses: "There are four different processes. Two of them are similar and the other two are different things." He says the first three were designed so cryptanalysis gives access to the English, and on the last one: "I masked the English language so it's more of a challenge now." — [Wired, "Inside Info on Kryptos' Codes," 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt (same interview): with K4 "you need to solve the technique first and then go for the puzzle." Asked whether all the processes are known ones, he replied, "The masking technique may not be known." — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt (same interview): "All four (sections) are done in the English language." This rules out a non-English K4 plaintext. — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt, by email to the NYT (2020-01-29): K4's 97-character length "could, in itself, present a decryption challenge." Per the NYT, he said the last passage uses "a masking technique, a further level of obfuscation." — [NYT interactive, 2020-01-29](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2020/01/29/climate/kryptos-sculpture-final-clue.html)
- Scheidt, quoted in the Baltimore Sun (2000-03-17): "There was a change in the methodology." The paper says the passages were made harder as the text progressed. — [Baltimore Sun, 2000-03-17](https://www.baltimoresun.com/2000/03/17/unlocking-the-secret-of-kryptos-cryptogram-for-nearly-a-decade-a-jumble-of-seemingly-random-letters-on-a-sculpture-at-cia-headquarters-has-mystified-experts-who-have-tried-to-decipher-its-code/). Wikipedia repeats this, along with a "nine out of ten" difficulty rating attributed to Scheidt, citing [The Conversation, 2021-05-30](https://theconversation.com/declassified-cold-war-code-breaking-manual-has-lessons-for-solving-impossible-puzzles-161595).
- Per NPR (2005-06-09), Scheidt said letter frequency, such as counting "E's vs. the number of Z's," helps on parts 1-3, and that in part 4 he deliberately masked that advantage. — [NPR transcript mirror on elonka.com, 2005-06-09](https://elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/npr/2005transcript.html)

**Sanborn modified Scheidt's systems; some "visual," individualistic systems**
- Sanborn (Wired, published 2005-01-19), on how many techniques Kryptos uses: "I would think five or six." — [Wired, "Questions for Kryptos' Creator," 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/0,1367,66333,00.html)
- Sanborn (same interview, full-page version): Scheidt gave him "a whole variety of possible systems… and ways to modify" them. He says he "did use some matrix codes Ed gave me." He also says he "designed visual systems for encoding," which he describes as harder to crack because they are "individualistic." — [Wired 2005, single-page version](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Sanborn (same interview) says Scheidt cannot decipher it: "I'd be modifying systems and developing my own." He calls the final part "the apex of the pyramid." — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Scheidt's reply (2005), as reported: "since he's the one who had the chisel in his hands, there could be some changes." This means Scheidt's recollection of the design may not match what was actually carved. — [Wired, "Solving the Enigma of Kryptos," p.3, 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66334,00.html)

**Not (necessarily) mathematical**
- Sanborn (Scientific American, 2025-11-12), asked about the math: "Who says it is even a math solution?" He also said he was "fortunate not to understand mathematics" in making the code, and urged "creativity." — [Scientific American, Dan Vergano, 2025-11-12](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)
- NYT (2020): Sanborn "makes no claims to mathematical accomplishment." — [NYT 2020-01-29](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2020/01/29/climate/kryptos-sculpture-final-clue.html)

**Earlier text points to later text**
- Sanborn (2005): "I have left instructions in the earlier text that refer to later text." — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Wired (2006) restates that Sanborn has said clues to the last section are in the earlier parts, so getting K1-K3 exactly right matters. — [Wired, "Typo Confounds Kryptos Sleuths," 2006-04-20](http://www.wired.com/news/technology/0,70701-0.html)

**Deliberate errors and misspellings**
- Sanborn (2005), on verifying a submitted K4 decipherment: "most of my things are rife with mistakes on purpose." — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Wired (2005): the encrypted sections contain spelling errors "which Sanborn said were intentional," and some characters are raised above the line. — [Wired 2005 p.2](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66334,00.html)
- NYT (2010): "Mr. Sanborn admitted to introducing misspellings to add a degree of difficulty." — [NYT 2010-11-21](http://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21code.html)
- NYT (2020): Sanborn says the IQLUSION misspelling was intentional, "to mix it up." — [NYT 2020](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2020/01/29/climate/kryptos-sculpture-final-clue.html)
- Elonka's FAQ: Sanborn says IQLUSION and UNDERGRUUND are deliberate but has not said why. The FAQ paraphrases him as saying their orientation or position matters more than the words. This is secondhand and undated. — [Elonka Kryptos FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)
- Unintentional error (2006): Sanborn removed an X at the end of a K2 line "for aesthetic reasons." That turned the accepted "ID BY ROWS" into "LAYER TWO." He said: "They didn't have everything to work with that I wanted them to have." — [Wired 2006-04-20 p.2](http://www.wired.com/news/technology/0,70701-1.html)

**The plaintext is not the end (riddle, location, K5)**
- Sanborn (2005), on solving: you have to "decipher the piece and then go to the agency and find that place." He says it refers to "an act that I could have carried out." — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Sanborn (2005): the answer will be "far more ambiguous" than the Cyrillic Projector's. — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Sanborn (2005): you do not need to be on the CIA grounds to solve the cipher ("No"). The physical location matters only for the riddle. — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Scheidt (2005): a readable message "may have other encoding that's involved in it." His example is a code word like "cake" standing for a meeting time. This implies the K4 plaintext may itself be a code. — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Sanborn (2010), on the BERLIN clue: "It doesn't mean you're going to understand it." — [Wired Threat Level, 2010-11-17](http://www.wired.com/threatlevel/2010/11/kryptos-clue/)
- "Riddle within a riddle," solvable only after all four passages are read. — [CNN, 2020-07-25](https://www.cnn.com/2020/07/25/us/kryptos-secret-message-code-trnd/index.html) (as cited by Wikipedia; not fetched directly)
- Sanborn open letter (Aug 2025): "its riddle will persist as K5." — [Elonka mirror of Open Letter, Aug 2025](https://elonka.com/kryptos/OpenLetterAug2025.html)
- K5 details (Nov 2025): K5 will be 97 characters, will share some coded words in the same positions as K4, and will appear in a "public space." — [Scientific American 2025-11-12](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)

**Content constraints Sanborn has given**
- Per SciAm's summary of his letter (2025): the plaintext involves Sanborn's 1986 Egypt trip and the 1989 fall of the Berlin Wall, and the codes concern "delivering a message." BERLINCLOCK refers to Berlin's World Clock (Weltzeituhr, Alexanderplatz). — [SciAm 2025-11-12](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)
- 2014 (NYT): "There are several really interesting clocks in Berlin" and "You'd better delve into that particular clock." The NYT tied this to Dieter Binninger, designer of the Berlin-Uhr / Mengenlehreuhr. — [NYT 2014-11-21](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html). Wired (2014) also says Sanborn was most intrigued by the Binninger Berlin Clock. — [Wired 2014-11-20](http://www.wired.com/2014/11/second-kryptos-clue/). **Conflict:** this does not match the 2025 statement that BERLINCLOCK means the World Clock.
- 2014: Sanborn said the fall of the Berlin Wall influenced him: "there's no doubt I was influenced." — [NYT 2014](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html)
- Length: Sanborn calls K4 97 characters. The FAQ notes the "?" before K4 might belong to it. Scheidt said in 2005, "I think there are 98 characters left." — [Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html); [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- From the CLOCK clue, ciphertext K at position 74 maps to plaintext K, so a letter can encrypt to itself. This is Wikipedia's observation, not a Sanborn statement. It rules out pure Enigma-style and other no-self-encipherment schemes for that position. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)

**Plaintext found ≠ solved (2025)**
- Sanborn (RR Auction post, 2025-10-23): "They did not solve K4 and they certainly did not find the key." And: "K4 has been discovered and it points in the direction of K5." — [RR Auction, 2025-10-23](https://content.rrauction.com/kryptos-k4-discovered-not-solved-heres-what-actually-happened/)
- Sanborn (Nov 2025): "K4 has not been solved or decrypted." — [SciAm 2025-11-12](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)
- Kobek: they "recovered the plaintext" and did not claim a cryptographic solve. — [Zona Motel interview, 2025-10-17](https://zonamotel.substack.com/p/interview-kryptos-k4-uncovered-jarett)
- Kobek reports that Sanborn told a dinner group in 2019 he "did not care" what method a solver used. This is Kobek's account, not a direct Sanborn quote. — [Wikipedia citing Zona Motel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos)

**Sanborn on the sealed "solution" envelope**
- Sanborn (2005): "I definitely didn't give him the last section." So the envelope given to Webster in 1990 does not hold the K4 answer. — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- Sanborn on NPR (2005): "I don't really believe I gave them the entire code." — [NPR transcript mirror, 2005-06-09](https://elonka.com/kryptos/mirrors/npr/2005transcript.html)

### Inferences
- Scheidt's account is "four processes; two similar, two different." K1 and K2 are the two similar Vigenère-type ciphers. K3 is transposition, and K4 is a fourth, distinct process with an extra masking layer. A plain Vigenère or plain columnar transposition of the K1-K3 type is therefore unlikely on its own. The NSA's 1992 analysis also failed with the K1-K3 combination (see Q4).
- Sanborn counted "five or six" techniques against Scheidt's "four processes." The gap suggests extra layers, such as masking or Sanborn's own "visual" systems, beyond the base ciphers.
- Sanborn talks of a "visual" system, a "not math" solution, and "creativity." Together these suggest at least one step depends on the sculpture's physical layout or appearance, which purely statistical attacks may not capture. Treat this as a soft constraint, not an exclusion.
- Intentional errors may exist inside K4 itself, per "rife with mistakes on purpose." A decryption that is close but not letter-perfect should not be discarded automatically. A negatives database should record whether each tested hypothesis allowed for errors.
- Scheidt's "cake" remark means the plaintext may contain code words. Plaintext-scoring functions that assume natural English throughout could reject the true plaintext.

### Gaps
- I found no verbatim Sanborn statement of the form "K4 is not a [named cipher]." Lists of excluded ciphers circulating online come from fan sources. I could not trace any of them to a primary Sanborn quote.
- I found no primary Sanborn statement specifically about the "?" between K3 and K4. The FAQ treats this as a Kryptos-group question.
- Steven Levy's 2025 Wired/Backchannel interview, the source of the AI quotes, was not fetched because wired.com is blocked. I used Boing Boing's quotation of it.
- I did not fetch Sanborn's 2025 Washington Post letter or the NYT articles from Oct 2025 and June 2026 directly.

## Q2. What does "masking" refer to per Sanborn/Scheidt, and what did Scheidt say about techniques?

### Takeaway
"Masking" is Scheidt's word, from 2005 onward and again in 2020. It means a step that hides K4's English letter-frequency statistics, so the technique has to be recovered before the text. Scheidt says the masking technique "may not be known," meaning it may not be a standard published system. He keeps it secret, and he never checked what Sanborn actually carved.

### Cited Findings
- Scheidt (2005): K1-K3 used "frequency counting and other techniques that are similar." In those, "the English language is still visible through the code," and in K4 "I disguise that." — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt (2005): "The masking technique may not be known." It is "part of the puzzle I told (Jim) I would keep secret." — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt (2005), on Sanborn's claim that he masked things further: "I haven't talked to Jim on what he did," but "there was some masking techniques that were used." Note the plural. — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt (2005): "We came up with a methodology using some of the known cryptographic solutions (at the time)." — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt (2005), on approaches: "Would you approach it mathematically?" or through "secret writings or symbols?" — [Wired 2005-01-21](http://www.wired.com/news/culture/0,1284,66359,00.html)
- Scheidt, quoted in the 2014 NYT: "But a technique that I used obviously worked." — [NYT 2014-11-21](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html)
- Scheidt (2020, by email): the last passage uses a "masking technique, a further level of obfuscation." — [NYT 2020-01-29](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2020/01/29/climate/kryptos-sculpture-final-clue.html)
- Sanborn's side (2005): he used "matrix codes Ed gave me" plus his own "visual systems for encoding." — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)
- CIA.gov: K1-K3 used a Vigenère-based "chart" system combined with "matrix coding." K4 uses a harder cipher and "has not been broken." — [CIA.gov, About the Kryptos Sculpture](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/)
- The academic matrix (Hill cipher) conjecture: Bauer, Link and Molle (Cryptologia, 2016) point to an extra "L" in the tableau and "HILL" down the rightmost column. — [Wikipedia citing Cryptologia 40(5)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos); [DOI 10.1080/01611194.2016.1141556](https://dx.doi.org/10.1080/01611194.2016.1141556)

### Inferences
- "Masking" most plausibly means a step that flattens frequencies before or after the main cipher. Candidates include a pre-substitution, nulls or a mixed-alphabet step, or a second layer. Scheidt never names it.
- Sanborn's phrase "matrix codes" could refer to the K3 transposition matrix or to Hill-style matrix encryption. The 2016 Hill conjecture builds on this ambiguity, so it is not confirmed by either man.

### Gaps
- No primary source defines the masking mechanism.
- The Baltimore Sun "change in the methodology" quote is from 2000. I saw it only through search snippets and did not fetch the full article.

## Q3. What did Sanborn say about submitted wrong solutions (volume, patterns, fee)?

### Takeaway
Sanborn has received "tens of thousands" of submissions. His gatekeeping has gone through several stages:
- **c.2010:** a friend checks a single letter position.
- **2010:** a website requiring the first 10 characters.
- **c.2012:** a $50 Western Union fee, with at most two emails per submission.
- **2020:** an email process with a $50 fee.
- **2025:** an AI auto-responder, handed to the auction buyer.

Nearly all submissions are wrong. As of 2010 no one had more than two letters right. Common patterns he names: conspiracy, Nazi and hostile entries after BERLIN, and since about 2024 a flood of confident AI/LLM "solutions."

### Cited Findings
- 2010: a friend handles claims by asking "What do you have in letter-position 27?" A mismatch means "Game over." "So far, no one has had more than two letters right." — [NYT 2010-11-21](http://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21code.html)
- 2010: claimants push back: "who are you to say I didn't crack the code?" Some suggest he doesn't know the answer himself. One claimant showed up at his home with a three-ring binder. — [NYT 2010](http://www.nytimes.com/2010/11/21/us/21code.html)
- Nov 2010: a new website requires claimants to submit what they believe are the first 10 characters of K4 before he responds. — [Wired 2010-11-17](http://www.wired.com/threatlevel/2010/11/kryptos-clue/); [Wired 2010-11-21](http://www.wired.com/threatlevel/2010/11/clue-kryptos/)
- 2014: the BERLIN clue led to "a tsunami" of entries, many "frivolous or debasing or hostile," plus messages from Nazi enthusiasts. — [NYT 2014-11-21](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html)
- Fee: from about 2012, "$50 fee (via Western Union)" for "no more than two back-and-forth-emails," with no extra clues. Money is refunded if he declines to respond. His verdict: "It's made it manageable." — [NYT 2014](http://www.nytimes.com/2014/11/21/us/another-kryptos-clue-is-offered-in-a-24-year-old-mystery-at-the-cia.html)
- 2020: an email-based process with a $50 fee. "He receives regular inquiries, so far none of them successful." Sanborn: "It's not something I thought I would be doing 30 years on." — [NYT 2020-01-29](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2020/01/29/climate/kryptos-sculpture-final-clue.html)
- 2025 (via Steven Levy): "The numbers [of submissions] have increased dramatically." He says AI users are "totally convinced that they cracked Kryptos during breakfast!" He told one solver: "AI lies, and does not have enough info." — [Boing Boing, 2025-03-08, quoting Levy/Wired](https://boingboing.net/2025/03/08/cia-kryptos-sculptor-disgusted-by-flood-of-wrong-ai-solutions.html)
- Open letter (Aug 2025): "tens of thousands of emails and letters" over the years. He also describes "a flood of meaningless decrypts" from AI. — [Open Letter, Aug 2025](https://elonka.com/kryptos/OpenLetterAug2025.html)
- Nov 2025: Sanborn built an AI system to answer claimed solutions automatically and will hand it to the auction winner. — [SciAm 2025-11-12](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/cia-kryptos-puzzle-creator-releases-final-clues/)
- 2026: Paradigm, the buyer at $962,500, disclosed itself and runs a hash-based public verifier plus a CTF. — [Wikipedia citing NYT 2026-06-12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kryptos) (NYT not fetched directly)
- Wired (2005): "In the early days, anything I said was a clue." Sanborn says he is careful because people hunt for "tiny little slivers of information." — [Wired 2005](http://www.wired.com/news/business/1,66333-0.html)

### Inferences
- In 2010 Sanborn's friend used "letter-position 27" as the check. Position 27 falls inside NORTHEAST (positions 26-34, released 2020), so the test letter is O. This is my inference, assuming the same 1-based indexing. It suggests that for a decade the check letter was one later publicly released, and no submitter had found it.
- "No one more than two letters right" (2010) implies early submissions were close to random. At about 1/26 per letter, two matches in 97 is chance level.

### Gaps
- No statistics on submission volume per year beyond "tens of thousands" in total.
- The exact start date of the $50 fee is given only as "two years ago" in Nov 2014, so about 2012.

## Q4. Which eliminations / dead ends do Elonka's FAQ, Wikipedia, CIA and NSA list?

### Takeaway
The curated sites list very few documented eliminations. Elonka's FAQ explicitly lists its "Has anyone thought of…" ideas as speculation, not tested negatives. Wikipedia lists no failed K4 approaches. The most concrete documented negative is the NSA's 1992 internal note: plaintext autokey and "transposed substitution" (the K1/K2 Vigenère combined with K3-style transposition) were tried on K4 without success. The scope of those attempts is not stated.

### Cited Findings (supporting detail)
- NSA 1992 K4 note: "There are only 97 characters remaining in section 4." It adds that because the 63-character K1 was broken, "a solution is certainly possible." It reports interval-7 roughness, "could be a characteristic of plaintext auto-key," and the hypothesis that K4 "employs both of the systems already used." It concludes: "There is no solution at the current time." — [NSA FOIA PDF](https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/controversies/KRYPTOS-Statue-NSA.pdf) (chronology in the same PDF: challenge relayed 1992; K1-K3 solved by three NSA analysts in 1992; formal letter to DIRNSA 1993)
- NSA recap: K1 = 10 alphabets, key PALIMPSEST; K2 = 8 alphabets, key ABSCISSA; K3 = keyed columnar transposition, 4×86 matrix, key KRYPTOS. All of this is K1-K3 only. — same PDF
- The Black Vault describes its NSA release as following a 2011 FOIA request, plus a 98-page CIA presentation declassified Sept 2014 and an Intellipedia entry released July 2017. — [Black Vault](https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/the-cias-kryptos-statue/)
- CIA.gov says only that K4 "has not been broken." It quotes Sanborn: "what I wrote is a mystery itself." It lists no failed methods. — [CIA.gov](https://www.cia.gov/legacy/headquarters/kryptos-sculpture/)

- Elonka FAQ full-credit criteria (FAQ's own, not Sanborn's): the plaintext must include the released cribs, and the method must be verifiable. — [Elonka FAQ](https://elonka.com/kryptos/faq.html)
- Elonka's main page lists no tested dead ends, only her own speculation on GPS coordinates and a manhole cover. It does flag the CIA transcript and CIA info page as containing errors. — [elonka.com/kryptos](https://elonka.com/kryptos/)

### Inferences
- For a negatives database, only items 1-2 (NSA), 4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 15 and 16 count as reliable negatives. The FAQ list (item 11) should be recorded as "tried informally, unverified."
- The NSA's 1992 failure with "Vigenère then transposition" is the oldest documented professional negative for the "combine K1-K3 methods" idea. It is consistent with Scheidt's statement that K4 is a distinct, masked process.
